Most of the leading open language models of 2026 are based on the mixture-of-experts (MoE) architecture. In an MoE layer, a router sends each token to only a few of many experts. Many of these models keep the experts equally busy with a bias for each expert, which falls when the expert receives too many tokens and rises when it receives too few. Most have also replaced the AdamW optimizer with Muon, which orthogonalizes the update of each weight matrix. However, the labs that build these models disagree on whether Muon should also train the router. Moreover, no published study has measured how the optimizer changes the work of the bias. We measure this work by the span, the difference between the largest and the smallest bias in a layer. We trained small MoE language models, with 55M and 108M active parameters, on educational web text. In 145 runs, we varied which optimizer trained each part of the network. Except for the tuning of the learning rates, we trained each configuration with three random seeds.

Muon made the bias work harder than AdamW\@. The span was higher under Muon at every seed, and more than twice as large in the first MoE layer. Halving the learning rate of Muon removed about three quarters of the extra span of this layer. Muon also reached a lower loss and made the experts more specialized. The span of the first MoE layer depended on the optimizer of the rest of the network, not on that of the router. With the rest of the network on Muon, the inputs of the first router shared a direction, through which the router favored the same experts for every token. The bias needed hundreds of steps to cancel this preference. Meanwhile, the busiest expert of the layer received four to seven times as many tokens as it would if the loads were even. When we removed this direction from the input of the first router for the first 3,000 steps, this overload and most of the extra span disappeared, and the loss did not change. With the router on AdamW, the loads of the experts shifted more early in training than with the router on Muon, and the loss was slightly higher. No run diverged, even at 16 times the tuned learning rates. The span stayed higher with a faster bias, with an added balance loss, and on the larger model.

For models of our size, these results favor keeping the router on Muon when the rest of the network is on Muon. They also point to the first MoE layer as the one to watch most closely.
